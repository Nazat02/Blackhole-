A rendered black hole, held to physics

Nothing gets out.
Not even the light behind it.

Perihelion renders a single stellar‑mass black hole the way general relativity actually bends it — the far side of its own accretion disk lensed up and over the shadow, into a ring. No artistic license. Every curve here comes from a geodesic, not a filter.

Most illustrations draw a flat dark circle with a ring floating beside it. That's wrong in a specific, checkable way: space itself is curved near this much mass, so light from behind the horizon gets pulled around it before it ever reaches your eye. What looks like decoration below — the second, fainter arc above the shadow — is that bent light, computed the same way it would be for any observer at this distance.

Why the disk shows up twice

Light doesn't travel in straight lines near this much mass. Photons leaving the far side of the accretion disk — the half you'd expect to be hidden behind the black hole — get bent over the top and bottom of the shadow before reaching you. You see that half of the disk twice: once directly, once lensed into a bright loop above and below the horizon.

The shadow itself is larger than the horizon. Because light circling near the photon sphere gets captured too, the dark disk you see is roughly 2.6 times the true event horizon radius — a detail most illustrations skip, and one the Event Horizon Telescope's 2019 image of M87* confirmed directly.

One side looks hotter than the other, and it isn't lighting. The disk rotates near the speed of light close to the horizon. Gas moving toward you gets relativistically beamed brighter and bluer; gas moving away dims and reddens. That asymmetry — not a light source — is why one edge of the ring glows harder in the render.

Questions worth asking before you trust a render

Is this a real black hole, or a composite?
Neither, exactly. There's no telescope data being piped in — it's a physically‑motivated approximation, tuned to match the qualitative features confirmed by real observations (the Event Horizon Telescope's 2019 image of M87*, and 2022's image of Sagittarius A*), not a pixel‑for‑pixel reproduction of either.
Why does one side of the ring look brighter?
Relativistic Doppler beaming. Gas on the side of the disk rotating toward you is moving close to the speed of light, which both brightens and blue‑shifts it in your direction. The receding side dims and reddens by the same mechanism. It's a real, predicted asymmetry — not a stylistic choice.
Why is "Rotation Speed" different from "Kerr Spin"?
Rotation Speed is cosmetic — how fast the disk's texture appears to swirl on screen. Kerr Spin (a*) is the actual general‑relativistic parameter that changes the shape of spacetime itself: the horizon size, the ergosphere, and where the innermost stable orbit sits. You can crank one without touching the other.
Could a black hole this size actually exist?
Yes — the default parameters here are deliberately set to M87*'s scale, a supermassive black hole about 6.5 billion times the Sun's mass, sitting at the center of the galaxy M87, roughly 55 million light‑years away.
